Probability review

Every Monte Carlo method in these notes ends the same way: we average \(N\) random samples and ask how close the average is to the number we want. This appendix collects the facts from probability and statistics that answer that question. We review random variables and the rules for their means and variances, and what it means to condition one random variable on another. We then use them to measure how good an estimator is, how fast a sample average converges, and what changes for nonlinear estimators and dependent samples.

Random variables

A random variable \(X\) is a numerical outcome of a random experiment. Its cumulative distribution function (CDF) is \[F_X(x) = P(X \le x).\]

A discrete random variable takes values \(x_1, x_2, \ldots\) with probabilities \(p_i = P(X = x_i)\), where \(\sum_i p_i = 1\). A continuous random variable has a density \(f_X \ge 0\) with \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_X(x)\,dx = 1\), and \[F_X(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x} f_X(t)\,dt, \qquad f_X(x) = F_X'(x).\]

The same definitions apply to a random vector \((X, Y)\), with a joint density \(f_{X,Y}(x, y)\) in place of \(f_X(x)\). The point \((X, Y)\) uniform on the square \([-1, 1]^2\) of Estimating \(\pi\) has density \(1/4\) on the square and \(0\) outside it.

Expectation and variance

Definition 1 (Expectation and variance) The expectation of \(g(X)\), for a function \(g\), is \[ \mathbb{E}[g(X)] = \begin{cases} \displaystyle\sum_i g(x_i)\, p_i & \text{if } X \text{ is discrete}, \\[1ex] \displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} g(x)\, f_X(x)\,dx & \text{if } X \text{ is continuous}. \end{cases} \] The variance of \(X\) is \[\text{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}\big[(X - \mathbb{E}[X])^2\big] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - \mathbb{E}[X]^2,\] and its standard deviation is \(\sqrt{\text{Var}(X)}\).

Two special cases recur throughout the notes: probabilities and integrals are expectations. For an event \(A\), the indicator \(\mathbf{1}_A\) is \(1\) when \(A\) happens and \(0\) otherwise, so \(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_A] = P(A)\); this is how the circle estimator writes \(\pi/4\) as an expectation. If \(U\) is uniform on \([a, b]\), then \(\mathbb{E}[g(U)] = \frac{1}{b - a}\int_a^b g(x)\,dx\); this is the starting point of Estimating integrals.

Independence and sums

Random variables \(X_1, \ldots, X_N\) are independent if their joint CDF factors, \(P(X_1 \le x_1, \ldots, X_N \le x_N) = \prod_i P(X_i \le x_i)\). They are i.i.d. if they are independent and share one distribution, as repeated draws from the same experiment do.

The covariance of \(X\) and \(Y\) is \[\text{Cov}(X, Y) = \mathbb{E}\big[(X - \mathbb{E}[X])(Y - \mathbb{E}[Y])\big],\] and independent random variables have covariance zero. Three rules compute almost every mean and variance in the notes.

ImportantMeans and variances of sums

For constants \(a\), \(b\) and random variables \(X\), \(Y\), \(X_1, \ldots, X_N\), expectation is always linear, \[\mathbb{E}[aX + bY] = a\,\mathbb{E}[X] + b\,\mathbb{E}[Y],\] variance scales as \(\text{Var}(aX + b) = a^2\,\text{Var}(X)\), and the variance of a sum is \[\text{Var}\Big(\sum_i X_i\Big) = \sum_i \text{Var}(X_i) + 2\sum_{i < j} \text{Cov}(X_i, X_j),\] which is \(\sum_i \text{Var}(X_i)\) when the \(X_i\) are independent.

Linearity holds even for dependent random variables. That is what makes Barbier’s noodle work (Estimating \(\pi\)): the pieces of a bent needle are dependent, but the expected number of crossings is still the sum of the pieces’ expectations. The variance rule needs independence, or the covariances.

Conditioning

Often we know the value of one random variable \(X\) and want to describe another, \(Y\), in light of it. For a random vector \((X, Y)\) with joint density \(f_{X,Y}\), the marginal density of \(X\) is \(f_X(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_{X,Y}(x, y)\,dy\), and the conditional density of \(Y\) given \(X = x\) is \[f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) = \frac{f_{X,Y}(x, y)}{f_X(x)},\] wherever \(f_X(x) > 0\). For each fixed \(x\) this is a density in \(y\): it is the slice of the joint density along the line \(X = x\), rescaled to integrate to \(1\). For discrete random variables the same formula holds with probabilities in place of densities, \(P(Y = y \mid X = x) = P(X = x, Y = y)/P(X = x)\). If \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent, the conditional density of \(Y\) is just \(f_Y\): knowing \(X\) tells us nothing about \(Y\).

Conditional expectation

Definition 2 (Conditional expectation and variance) The conditional expectation of \(Y\) given \(X = x\) is the mean of the conditional distribution, \[m(x) = \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X = x] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} y\, f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x)\,dy,\] with a sum in the discrete case. The conditional expectation of \(Y\) given \(X\) is the random variable \[\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X] = m(X).\] The conditional variance of \(Y\) given \(X\) is the random variable \[\text{Var}(Y \mid X) = \mathbb{E}\big[(Y - \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X])^2 \,\big|\, X\big] = \mathbb{E}[Y^2 \mid X] - \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X]^2.\]

Notice the two steps. For a fixed number \(x\), \(\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X = x]\) is a number, so \(m\) is an ordinary function. We then plug the random variable \(X\) into it. So \(\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X]\) is random, but its only randomness is that of \(X\): it is what is left of \(Y\) once everything except \(X\) has been averaged out. In the same way \(\text{Var}(Y \mid X)\) is a function of \(X\), namely the variance of \(Y\) that remains once \(X\) is known.

For example, let us roll two dice \(D_1\) and \(D_2\), and let \(S = D_1 + D_2\). Given \(D_1 = x\), the sum is \(x\) plus a fresh die roll, which has mean \(7/2\) and variance \(35/12\). So \[\mathbb{E}[S \mid D_1] = D_1 + \tfrac72, \qquad \text{Var}(S \mid D_1) = \tfrac{35}{12}.\] The first is random, and takes the values \(4.5, 5.5, \ldots, 9.5\) with probability \(1/6\) each. The second happens to be the same number whatever \(D_1\) is.

A second example is the hit-or-miss estimator of Estimating integrals. There \((X, Y)\) is uniform in a box \([a, b] \times [0, c]\), and the hit \(H\) is \(1\) if \(Y \le g(X)\) and \(0\) otherwise, for a curve \(0 \le g \le c\). Given \(X = x\), the height \(Y\) is still uniform on \([0, c]\), so \(H\) is a \(\text{Bernoulli}\) coin with success probability \(g(x)/c\). So \[\mathbb{E}[H \mid X] = \frac{g(X)}{c}, \qquad \text{Var}(H \mid X) = \frac{g(X)}{c}\Big(1 - \frac{g(X)}{c}\Big),\] and this time both depend on \(X\).

Conditional expectation is linear, just like expectation, and it has two rules of its own. A function of \(X\) is known once \(X\) is, so it behaves like a constant, \(\mathbb{E}[g(X)\,Y \mid X] = g(X)\,\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X]\). And if \(Y\) is independent of \(X\), then \(\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X] = \mathbb{E}[Y]\). Both were used for the dice: \(\mathbb{E}[D_1 + D_2 \mid D_1] = D_1 + \mathbb{E}[D_2]\).

The laws of total expectation and total variance

Since \(\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X]\) and \(\text{Var}(Y \mid X)\) are random variables, we can take their means and variances. These give back the mean and variance of \(Y\).

ImportantTotal expectation and total variance

For random variables \(X\) and \(Y\), the law of total expectation is \[\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X]\big] = \mathbb{E}[Y],\] and the law of total variance is \[\text{Var}(Y) = \text{Var}\big(\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X]\big) + \mathbb{E}\big[\text{Var}(Y \mid X)\big].\]

The first law says that we may compute a mean in two stages: average \(Y\) for each value of \(X\), then average over \(X\). It follows from the definitions, since \(\int m(x)\, f_X(x)\,dx = \iint y\, f_{X,Y}(x, y)\,dy\,dx\).

For the second law, write \(m(X) = \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X]\) and split \(Y - \mathbb{E}[Y] = (Y - m(X)) + (m(X) - \mathbb{E}[Y])\). Squaring and taking expectations, the cross term vanishes: given \(X\), the factor \(m(X) - \mathbb{E}[Y]\) is a constant, and \(Y - m(X)\) has conditional mean zero. The two squares that remain are \(\mathbb{E}[\text{Var}(Y \mid X)]\) and \(\text{Var}(m(X))\). So the variance of \(Y\) splits into the part that \(X\) explains, and the part that is left once \(X\) is known. For the dice, \(\text{Var}(D_1 + \tfrac72) + \tfrac{35}{12} = \tfrac{35}{12} + \tfrac{35}{12} = \tfrac{35}{6}\), which is indeed the variance of a sum of two dice.

Both terms of the second law are nonnegative, so \[\text{Var}\big(\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X]\big) \le \text{Var}(Y).\] In other words, replacing a random variable by its conditional expectation keeps its mean and can only lower its variance. This is why the sample mean beats hit-or-miss in Estimating integrals, and it is a general way to improve an estimator: whatever part of the randomness we can average out exactly, we should.

Estimators

An estimator of an unknown number \(\theta\) is a quantity \(\hat\theta_N\) computed from \(N\) random samples. It is itself a random variable: run the experiment again and you get a different value. Monte Carlo’s main estimator is the sample mean.

Definition 3 (The sample mean) Let \(X_1, \ldots, X_N\) be i.i.d. copies of a random variable \(X\), and let \(\theta = \mathbb{E}[g(X)]\) for a function \(g\). The sample mean estimator of \(\theta\) is \[\hat\theta_N = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} g(X_i).\]

The circle estimator of \(\pi\) is \(4\) times a sample mean, with \(g(X, Y) = \mathbf{1}_{\{X^2 + Y^2 \le 1\}}\).

Bias, standard error and MSE

Three numbers measure how far an estimator is from \(\theta\).

Definition 4 (Bias, standard error, MSE) For an estimator \(\hat\theta_N\) of \(\theta\), the bias is \(\text{Bias}(\hat\theta_N) = \mathbb{E}[\hat\theta_N] - \theta\), and \(\hat\theta_N\) is unbiased if the bias is \(0\). The standard error is \(\text{SE}(\hat\theta_N) = \sqrt{\text{Var}(\hat\theta_N)}\), and the mean squared error is \(\text{MSE}(\hat\theta_N) = \mathbb{E}\big[(\hat\theta_N - \theta)^2\big]\).

The MSE combines the other two. Expanding \(\hat\theta_N - \theta = (\hat\theta_N - \mathbb{E}[\hat\theta_N]) + \text{Bias}(\hat\theta_N)\) and squaring, the cross term has expectation zero, so \[\text{MSE}(\hat\theta_N) = \text{Var}(\hat\theta_N) + \text{Bias}(\hat\theta_N)^2.\] For an unbiased estimator the MSE is the variance, and the standard error is the natural measure of uncertainty. For a biased one, the MSE is.

The rules for sums give the sample mean’s bias and standard error directly. Write \(\sigma^2 = \text{Var}(g(X))\). Linearity gives \(\mathbb{E}[\hat\theta_N] = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i \mathbb{E}[g(X_i)] = \theta\), so the sample mean is unbiased, and independence gives \(\text{Var}(\hat\theta_N) = \frac{1}{N^2}\sum_i \text{Var}(g(X_i)) = \sigma^2/N\).

ImportantThe \(N^{-1/2}\) rate

The sample mean of \(N\) i.i.d. samples with finite variance \(\sigma^2\) is unbiased, with \[\text{SE}(\hat\theta_N) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt N}.\] The error falls like \(N^{-1/2}\) whatever the problem, and whatever its dimension; only the constant \(\sigma\) depends on the problem. Halving the error costs four times as many samples, and one more correct decimal digit costs a hundred times as many.

The standard error is in the units of \(\theta\), so whether \(0.01\) is small depends on the size of \(\theta\). Dividing by \(\theta\) removes the units.

Definition 5 (Relative standard error) For an estimator \(\hat\theta_N\) of \(\theta \ne 0\), the relative standard error is \[\text{RSE}(\hat\theta_N) = \frac{\text{SE}(\hat\theta_N)}{|\theta|}.\]

A relative standard error of \(0.01\) means a typical error of 1% of \(\theta\). For the sample mean it is \(\sigma/(|\theta|\sqrt N)\), so a relative standard error of \(\varepsilon\) takes \[N = \frac{\sigma^2}{\theta^2\,\varepsilon^2}\] samples. The rate is still \(N^{-1/2}\), but the constant is now \(\sigma/|\theta|\), and it is large when \(\theta\) is small compared to \(\sigma\). The standard example is a small probability \(p = P(A)\), estimated by the fraction of samples in \(A\). Here \(\sigma^2 = p(1 - p)\), so \[\text{RSE}(\hat p_N) = \sqrt{\frac{1 - p}{p\,N}} \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{p\,N}},\] and we need about \(1/(p\,\varepsilon^2)\) samples: the standard error shrinks as \(p \to 0\), while the relative standard error grows without bound. Estimating integrals meets this in high dimensions.

So the two ways to make a Monte Carlo estimate more accurate are more samples, which is slow, and a smaller \(\sigma\), which is the subject of Variance reduction.

Limit theorems

The standard error says how large the error typically is. Two theorems say more: the error goes to zero, and for large \(N\) its distribution is normal.

Theorem 1 (Strong law of large numbers) Let \(X_1, X_2, \ldots\) be i.i.d. with \(\mathbb{E}[|X_1|] < \infty\) and \(\mathbb{E}[X_1] = \mu\). Then, with probability \(1\), \[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} X_i \to \mu \quad \text{as } N \to \infty.\]

An estimator \(\hat\theta_N\) with \(\hat\theta_N \to \theta\) is called consistent. The law of large numbers says the sample mean is consistent as soon as \(\mathbb{E}[|g(X)|] < \infty\), even when \(\sigma^2\) is infinite. It gives no rate.

Theorem 2 (Central limit theorem) Let \(X_1, X_2, \ldots\) be i.i.d. with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(0 < \sigma^2 < \infty\), and let \(\bar X_N = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} X_i\). Then, for every \(z\), \[P\left(\frac{\bar X_N - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt N} \le z\right) \to \Phi(z) \quad \text{as } N \to \infty,\] where \(\Phi\) is the CDF of the standard normal distribution \(\mathcal{N}(0, 1)\).

In words: for large \(N\), the sample mean is approximately \(\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2/N)\). The variance \(\sigma^2/N\) is exact for every \(N\); the theorem adds that the shape becomes normal.

Remark 1 (Infinite variance). The central limit theorem needs \(\sigma^2 < \infty\). Without it, the sample mean can still converge (by Theorem 1) but slower than \(N^{-1/2}\), and without a normal limit. For example, if \(U\) is uniform on \((0, 1)\), then \(g(U) = U^{-2/3}\) has finite mean \(3\) but infinite variance. Estimating \(\int_0^1 x^{-2/3}\,dx = 3\) by a sample mean converges, but error bars computed from \(\sigma/\sqrt N\) are meaningless. Importance sampling is one way out.

Confidence intervals

The central limit theorem turns the standard error into a statement with a probability. For large \(N\), \[P\left(|\hat\theta_N - \theta| \le z\,\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt N}\right) \approx P(|Z| \le z) = 2\Phi(z) - 1, \qquad Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1).\]

The standard deviation \(\sigma\) is usually as unknown as \(\theta\) itself. We replace it by the sample standard deviation \(S_N\), the square root of the sample variance \[S_N^2 = \frac{1}{N - 1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big(g(X_i) - \hat\theta_N\big)^2.\] The factor \(1/(N - 1)\) makes \(S_N^2\) unbiased for \(\sigma^2\). Since \(S_N \to \sigma\), replacing \(\sigma\) by \(S_N\) does not change the limit.

Definition 6 (Confidence interval) An approximate \((1 - \alpha)\) confidence interval for \(\theta\) is \[\left[\hat\theta_N - z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\frac{S_N}{\sqrt N},\; \hat\theta_N + z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\frac{S_N}{\sqrt N}\right],\] where \(z_{1-\alpha/2} = \Phi^{-1}(1 - \alpha/2)\). For a 95% interval, \(z_{0.975} \approx 1.96\).

The interval is random; \(\theta\) is not. A 95% interval means that if we repeat the experiment many times, about 95% of the intervals contain \(\theta\). The half-width \(1.96\,S_N/\sqrt N\) also tells us how many samples we need: for a half-width \(\varepsilon\), about \[N \approx \left(\frac{1.96\,\sigma}{\varepsilon}\right)^2,\] with \(\sigma\) estimated from a small pilot run.

The delta method

Many estimators are not averages but smooth functions \(g(\hat p)\) of an average \(\hat p\) of mean \(p\): Buffon’s estimator of \(\pi\) is \(\frac{2\ell}{t}\cdot\frac{1}{\hat p}\). Since \(g\) is not linear, in general \[\mathbb{E}[g(\hat p)] \ne g(\mathbb{E}[\hat p]),\] so \(g(\hat p)\) is biased. A Taylor expansion of \(g\) about \(p\) approximates both its bias and its variance.

For the bias, expand to second order: \(g(\hat p) \approx g(p) + g'(p)(\hat p - p) + \tfrac12 g''(p)(\hat p - p)^2\). Taking expectations, the linear term vanishes, and \[\text{Bias}(g(\hat p)) \approx \tfrac12\, g''(p)\,\text{Var}(\hat p).\] Since \(\text{Var}(\hat p)\) is of order \(1/N\), the bias shrinks like \(1/N\). Its sign is the sign of \(g''\): a convex \(g\) overestimates on average (Jensen’s inequality, \(\mathbb{E}[g(\hat p)] \ge g(\mathbb{E}[\hat p])\)).

For the variance, the first order is enough. This is the delta method: \[\text{Var}(g(\hat p)) \approx g'(p)^2\,\text{Var}(\hat p), \qquad \text{SE}(g(\hat p)) \approx |g'(p)|\,\text{SE}(\hat p).\]

So the bias is of order \(1/N\), while the standard error is of order \(N^{-1/2}\). For large \(N\) the squared bias is negligible in the MSE, and the error still falls at the \(N^{-1/2}\) rate, with the constant multiplied by \(|g'(p)|\). Estimating \(\pi\) works this out for Buffon’s needle.

Dependent samples

Markov chain Monte Carlo produces samples \(X_1, X_2, \ldots\) that are identically distributed (in the long run) but not independent: each sample is a small step from the previous one. Linearity still makes their average unbiased in the long run, but the variance of a sum now includes the covariances, and positively correlated samples have a larger variance than independent ones, \[\text{Var}(\bar X_N) = \frac{\sigma^2}{N}\Big(1 + \frac{2}{N}\sum_{i < j}\text{Corr}(X_i, X_j)\Big).\] The effective sample size is the number of independent samples that would give the same variance. A strongly correlated chain of length \(N\) can be worth far fewer than \(N\) independent samples; Markov chains and the MCMC chapters measure this.

Summary

  • In Random variables we reviewed distributions, expectation and variance, saw that probabilities and integrals are expectations, and collected the rules for means and variances of sums. Linearity needs no independence; the variance of a sum drops its covariances only for independent terms.
  • In Conditioning we defined \(\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X]\) and \(\text{Var}(Y \mid X)\) as random variables that are functions of \(X\), and saw that the variance of \(Y\) splits into \(\text{Var}(\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X]) + \mathbb{E}[\text{Var}(Y \mid X)]\). So conditioning keeps the mean and can only lower the variance.
  • In Estimators we measured an estimator by its bias, standard error and mean squared error, with \(\text{MSE} = \text{Var} + \text{Bias}^2\), and showed that the sample mean is unbiased with standard error \(\sigma/\sqrt N\), whatever the dimension. The relative standard error \(\text{SE}/|\theta|\) measures the error against the size of \(\theta\), and is large when \(\theta\) is small compared to \(\sigma\).
  • In Limit theorems the law of large numbers made the sample mean consistent, and the central limit theorem made it approximately normal, which, with \(S_N\) in place of \(\sigma\), gave confidence intervals. With infinite variance the theorem and the intervals fail.
  • In The delta method a Taylor expansion of a nonlinear estimator \(g(\hat p)\) gave a bias of order \(1/N\) and a standard error of about \(|g'(p)|\,\text{SE}(\hat p)\), so the rate stays \(N^{-1/2}\).
  • In Dependent samples we saw that positive correlations inflate the variance of an average, and that the effective sample size counts how many independent samples a chain is worth.
