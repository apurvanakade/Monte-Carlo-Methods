Estimating Integrals

How would you compute \[ \int_{[0,1]^{20}} e^{-\|x\|^2}\,dx, \] an integral over a cube in twenty dimensions? The simplest method is to lay down a grid and add up the values of the integrand on it. But a grid with only ten points along each axis has \(10^{20}\) points, and even at a billion function evaluations a second the sum would take three thousand years! By contrast, just thirty thousand random points give the answer to about 1% accuracy. This is the Monte Carlo principle at work again.

In this chapter we will see why grids break down as the dimension grows, and how random points get around the problem. We build two estimators out of random points and ask how accurate each one is, which turns out to be a question about variance. We end with a brief look at quasi-random points, which try to combine the evenness of a grid with the flexibility of random points.

// "10⁻³" for 0.001: tick labels for a log axis whose ticks are powers of ten.
function intPowerOfTen(value) {
  const superscripts = {"-": "⁻", "0": "⁰", "1": "¹", "2": "²", "3": "³", "4": "⁴", "5": "⁵", "6": "⁶", "7": "⁷", "8": "⁸", "9": "⁹"}
  let exponent = ""
  for (const ch of String(Math.round(Math.log10(value)))) exponent += superscripts[ch]
  return `10${exponent}`
}

// Every power of ten from `lo` to `hi`.
function intDecades(lo, hi) {
  const ticks = []
  for (let k = Math.ceil(Math.log10(lo) - 1e-9); k <= Math.floor(Math.log10(hi) + 1e-9); k++) ticks.push(10 ** k)
  return ticks
}

// A log axis over [lo, hi] with a tick at each power of ten inside it.
function intLogAxis(lo, hi, options) {
  return {type: "log", domain: [lo, hi], ticks: intDecades(lo, hi), tickFormat: intPowerOfTen, ...options}
}

Grids and the curse of dimensionality

Let us start in one dimension, where we want to compute \[ I = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx. \] Perhaps the simplest numerical method for this is the midpoint rule. We cut \([a, b]\) into \(N\) cells of width \(h = (b-a)/N\), and on each cell we replace \(f\) by its value at the center \(c_j = a + (j - \tfrac12)h\) of the cell. Adding up the areas of the resulting rectangles gives \[ M_N = \frac{b-a}{N}\sum_{j=1}^N f(c_j). \] Figure 1 shows these rectangles for \(\sin x\) on \([0, \pi]\).

  1. Expand \(f(c + t)\) to second order about the center \(c\) of one cell and integrate over \(-h/2 \le t \le h/2\) to show \[ \int_{c-h/2}^{c+h/2} f(x)\,dx = h\,f(c) + \frac{h^3}{24}\,f''(c) + O(h^5). \] Why does the first-order term contribute nothing? Why is the remainder \(O(h^5)\) rather than \(O(h^4)\)?
  2. Sum over the cells to conclude \[ I - M_N = \frac{h^2}{24}\,\big(f'(b) - f'(a)\big) + O(h^4). \]
  3. Using (b), write the error of the midpoint rule as a power of \(N\). By what factor does it shrink when \(N\) doubles? How many times more points does one more correct decimal place cost? Compare with the standard-error exercise of the last chapter.

  4. What happens if \(f\) is convex, \(f'' \ge 0\)?

Exercise 3 (The midpoint rule for \(\sin x\))  

  1. For \(\sin x\) on \([0, \pi]\), evaluate the leading term \(\frac{h^2}{24}\big(f'(b) - f'(a)\big)\) of Exercise 1 (b) at \(N = 100\), where \(h = (b-a)/N\) is the cell width. Does its sign agree with your answer to Exercise 2?
  2. How many points does the midpoint rule need for the error in (a) to fall to \(0.001\)?

You should find that the error of the midpoint rule falls like \(N^{-2}\), so one more correct decimal place costs only about 3.2 times as many points. By comparison, the circle estimator of the last chapter requires 100 times as many computations for each extra decimal place. You can watch the rule at work on Visual Math Lab. So in one dimension, Riemann sums (and their optimizations) are fast enough for computing integrals.

The curse of dimensionality

The trouble starts when we move to higher dimensions. Let \(f\) now be a function on the unit cube \([0, 1]^d\), and suppose we want \[ I = \int_{[0,1]^d} f(x)\,dx. \] The midpoint rule generalizes directly. We cut each of the \(d\) axes into \(m\) cells of width \(h = 1/m\), which cuts the cube into \(N = m^d\) small cubes, and we replace \(f\) on each small cube by its value at the center. Since the cube has volume 1, the estimate \(M_N\) is simply the average of \(f\) over the \(N\) centers.

How accurate is it? Repeating the argument of Exercise 1 along each axis gives one error term per axis, \[ I - M_N \approx \frac{h^2}{24}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\int_{[0,1]^d}\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_k^2}(x)\,dx. \] Since \(h = 1/m = N^{-1/d}\), the error falls like \(N^{-2/d}\).

Exercise 4 (The curse of dimensionality) The product midpoint rule on the cube \([0, 1]^d\) uses \(m\) points along each axis, \(N = m^d\) in all.

  1. In dimension \(d\), how many times more points does one more correct decimal place cost? Evaluate your answer for \(d = 1\), \(2\), \(10\) and \(20\).
  2. The integral of the opening paragraph has \(d = 20\). How many points does the grid with \(m = 10\) have? At \(10^9\) evaluations of \(f\) a second, how long does the sum take?
  3. Halving the cell width divides the error by 4. By what factor does it multiply the running time in (b)?
  4. The coarsest grid that can see any variation along an axis has \(m = 2\). How many points does it have for \(d = 100\)?
ImportantThe curse of dimensionality

A grid rule whose error is \(m^{-r}\) per axis has error \(N^{-r/d}\) in terms of the total number of points \(N = m^d\). The rate gets worse as the dimension grows: one more correct decimal place costs \(10^{d/r}\) times as many points.

Figure 3 shows the curse of dimensionality for the integral we started with, \(e^{-\|x\|^2}\) over \([0, 1]^d\): on the log-log plot the errors fall along lines of slope \(-2/d\), which get flatter and flatter as \(d\) grows.

Hit or miss

Can random points do better? We have already seen one method that does not use a grid. In the last chapter, the circle estimator threw random points into a square and counted how many landed in the disk, using the fact that the area of the disk is the area of the square times the probability of landing in it. But an integral is an area too, namely the area under a graph, so the same idea should work here. Suppose \(0 \le f \le c\) on \([a, b]\). We draw a point \((X, Y)\) uniformly from the box \([a, b] \times [0, c]\) and record the hit \[ H = \mathbf{1}_{\{Y \le f(X)\}}, \] which is 1 when the point lands under the curve, and 0 otherwise.

Definition 1 (The hit-or-miss estimator) Given \(N\) independent uniform points \((X_i, Y_i)\) in the box \([a, b] \times [0, c]\), with hits \(H_i\), estimate \(I\) by \[\hat I_N^{\,\text{HM}} = c\,(b-a)\cdot\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} H_i = \text{box area} \times \text{fraction of points under the curve}.\]

  3. Take \(f(x) = \sin x\) on \([0, \pi]\) with \(c = 1\), so \(I = 2\). Compute the standard error, and the smallest \(N\) for which it is at most \(0.001\). Compare with Exercise 3 (b).
  4. Take \(f(x) = 4\sqrt{1 - x^2}\) on \([0, 1]\) with \(c = 4\), so \(I = \pi\). Check that you recover the standard error of the circle estimator from the last chapter.
  5. The height \(c\) of the box is your choice, as long as \(f \le c\). How does choosing a larger \(c\) affect \(\hat I_N^{\,\text{HM}}\)? Which \(c\) is best?
  6. Check (e) in the app: for \(\sin x\) at \(N = 2000\), compare Standard error at \(c = 1\) and at \(c = 4\) with your formula from (b).

You should find that we get the \(N^{-1/2}\) rate again, with a constant that gets worse the more of the box lies above the curve. Raising \(c\) in the app shows why: a point that lands above the curve tells us nothing about \(f\), and a taller box wastes more of its points up there. For \(\sin x\) the standard error is about \(1.51/\sqrt N\), so three correct decimal places cost about 2.3 million points. The midpoint rule needs only 29! In one dimension, random points are simply a poor choice, just as they were for computing \(\pi\).

Hit or miss in many dimensions

So why bother with random points at all? The reason is that hit-or-miss works in exactly the same way in any dimension. Let \(f\) be a function on the unit cube \([0, 1]^d\) with \(0 \le f \le c\), and let \[ I = \int_{[0,1]^d} f(x)\,dx. \] We draw \(X\) uniformly from the cube, which just means drawing each of its \(d\) coordinates independently and uniformly, and we draw \(Y\) uniformly from \([0, c]\). As before, we record a hit \(H = 1\) when \(Y \le f(X)\), and we estimate \(I\) by \(c\) times the fraction of hits, \[ \hat I_N^{\,\text{HM}} = \frac{c}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} H_i. \]

Exercise 6 (The rate does not see the dimension)  

  1. Show that \(\hat I_N^{\,\text{HM}}\) is unbiased with standard error \(\sqrt{I\,(c - I)/N}\). Where does the dimension \(d\) appear in your argument?
  2. Compare with the \(N^{-2/d}\) rate of the product midpoint rule. For which dimensions \(d\) does hit-or-miss have the better rate?

You should find that the dimension does not appear anywhere in the argument. The rate is \(N^{-1/2}\) in every dimension, and it beats the midpoint rule’s \(N^{-2/d}\) as soon as \(d > 4\). This is the reason why random sampling is the standard tool for high-dimensional integrals, even though it is nearly useless for one-dimensional ones.

Have we escaped the curse of dimensionality, then? Not quite. The rate does not depend on the dimension, but the constant in front of it does, and it can be enormous. A good example is the volume \(V_d\) of the unit ball in \(d\) dimensions. We can estimate it just as we estimated the area of the disk: draw uniform points in the cube \([-1, 1]^d\), which has volume \(2^d\), call a point a hit if it lands in the ball, and multiply the fraction of hits by \(2^d\). To keep the formulas simple, let us only look at even dimensions \(d = 2k\). The exact value is then known to be \(V_{2k} = \pi^k/k!\), so we can check our estimates.

Exercise 7 (The ball in the cube) Let \(V_d\) be the volume of the unit ball in \(d\) dimensions, and let \(p_d = V_d/2^d\) be the probability that a uniform point of the cube \([-1, 1]^d\) lands in the ball. The relative standard error of an estimator is its standard error divided by the quantity it estimates.

  1. Compute \(p_d\) for \(d = 2\), \(d = 10\) and \(d = 20\). How does it behave as \(d\) grows?
  2. Find the relative standard error of the hit-or-miss estimator of \(V_d\) with \(N\) points, in terms of \(p_d\) and \(N\). For each \(d\) in (a), find the smallest \(N\) for which it is at most \(0.1\).
  3. Take \(d = 20\) and draw \(N = 10^5\) points. How many hits do you expect? What does the estimator return when there are none, and what is its estimated standard error then?

You should find that the fraction of the cube filled by the ball shrinks faster than exponentially in \(d\), and the number of points we need grows just as fast: 28 points at \(d = 2\), about 40,000 at \(d = 10\), and about \(4 \times 10^9\) at \(d = 20\). In twenty dimensions, 100,000 points give about \(0.0025\) hits on average, so we should expect every one of them to miss the ball. The estimate is then 0, and worse, the estimated standard error is also 0, so the method reports a wrong answer with complete confidence! What is going on is that in high dimensions nearly all of the volume of a cube sits in its corners, far from the ball, and so uniform points spend almost all of their time where the integrand vanishes.

So even though the rate does not depend on the dimension, the number of points we need still does: it grows like \(1/p_d\). One way out is to sample more often where the integrand is large and then correct for this, an idea called importance sampling, which we will study in Variance reduction. Another is to make better use of the samples we already have, and this is what we turn to next.

The sample-mean estimator

There is a more direct way to write \(I = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx\) as an expectation. Recall the law of the unconscious statistician: if \(X\) has density \(p\), then \[\mathbb{E}[f(X)] = \int f(x)\,p(x)\,dx.\] If \(X\) is uniform on \([a, b]\), then \(p(x) = 1/(b-a)\) on the interval, and so \[\mathbb{E}[f(X)] = \frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \frac{I}{b-a}.\] In other words, the integral is the width of the interval times the average height of \(f\). The same is true in any dimension:

ImportantAn integral is an expectation

If \(X\) is uniformly distributed on a region \(D\) of volume \(|D|\), then \[\int_D f(x)\,dx = |D|\;\mathbb{E}[f(X)],\] so an average of \(f\) over random points of \(D\), times \(|D|\), estimates the integral.

This is exactly the kind of statement the Monte Carlo principle asks for. Replacing the expectation by a sample average gives our second estimator, which needs neither a box nor the extra coordinate \(Y\).

Definition 2 (The sample-mean estimator) Given \(N\) independent uniform points \(X_1, \ldots, X_N\) in \([a, b]\), estimate \(I = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx\) by \[\hat I_N = \frac{b-a}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} f(X_i).\]

Notice that \(\hat I_N = \sum_i \frac{b-a}{N}\,f(X_i)\) is the total area of \(N\) bars, each of width \((b-a)/N\). So this is a Riemann sum, just like the midpoint rule, except that the heights are measured at random points instead of at the centers of the cells. Figure 4 shows it for \(\sin x\) on \([0, \pi]\): the points are sorted from left to right, and the \(i\)-th bar gets the height of the \(i\)-th point, which is usually not inside its own bar.

intSamplePoints = 6

Once again our estimator is an average of i.i.d. samples, so we already know how to analyze it. Let us write \[ \sigma_f^2 = \text{Var}(f(X)) = \frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f(x)^2\,dx - \left(\frac{I}{b-a}\right)^2 \] for the variance of the height of \(f\) at a uniform point \(X\) of \([a, b]\).

Exercise 8 (Bias and standard error) Let \(X_1, \ldots, X_N\) be independent uniform points in \([a, b]\), and let \(\hat I_N = \frac{b-a}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N f(X_i)\) be the sample-mean estimator of \(I = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx\) (Definition 2). Write \(\sigma_f^2 = \text{Var}(f(X_i))\).

  1. Show that \(\hat I_N\) is unbiased.
  2. Compute \(\text{Var}(\hat I_N)\) and \(\text{SE}(\hat I_N)\) in terms of \(\sigma_f\). Where do you use independence?
  3. Take \(f(x) = \sin x\) on \([0, \pi]\), so \(I = 2\). Compute \(\sigma_f^2\) and \(\text{SE}(\hat I_N)\).
  4. Find the smallest \(N\) for which \(\text{SE}(\hat I_N) \le 0.001\) in (c).

You should find \[ \text{SE}(\hat I_N) = \frac{(b-a)\,\sigma_f}{\sqrt N}, \] which is the \(N^{-1/2}\) rate once more. Notice that the constant depends on \(f\) only through \(\sigma_f\). For \(\sin x\) the standard error is about \(0.967/\sqrt N\), so three correct decimal places cost about a million points.

There is one new difficulty. For hit-or-miss the samples were Bernoulli, so once we had estimated their mean we also knew their variance. Here \(\sigma_f\) is a second unknown integral. In practice we estimate it from the same samples, using their sample standard deviation \[ s = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{i=1}^N \big(f(X_i) - \bar f\,\big)^2}, \qquad \bar f = \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N f(X_i), \] and we report \((b-a)\,s/\sqrt N\) as the standard error.

Exercise 9 (Confidence intervals)  

  1. Write an approximate 95% confidence interval for \(I = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx\) centered at the sample-mean estimator \(\hat I_N\), for large \(N\), that uses only the samples \(f(X_1), \ldots, f(X_N)\).
  2. Use Exercise 8 (c) to predict the standard error for \(\sin x\) at \(N = 100\).

Hit or miss against the sample mean

We now have two unbiased estimators of the same integral, and they even use the same points \(X_i\). Is there any reason to choose one over the other? Both have the \(N^{-1/2}\) rate, so any difference must be in the constants. Let us first compare them on an example, and then ask whether what we see there is an accident.

Exercise 10 (Hit or miss against the sample mean) Let \(I = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx\) with \(0 \le f \le c\). With \(N\) points each, let \(\hat I_N^{\,\text{HM}}\) be the hit-or-miss estimator of \(I\) with box height \(c\) (Definition 1), and \(\hat I_N\) the sample-mean estimator (Definition 2).

  1. Take \(f(x) = \sin x\) on \([0, \pi]\) with \(c = 1\). Use Exercise 5 (c) and Exercise 8 (c) to compute the standard error of each estimator at \(N = 100\). Which is smaller? How many times more points does the other one need to match it?
  2. Is (a) special to \(\sin x\)? Use Exercise 5 (b) and Exercise 8 (b) to write \(\text{Var}(\hat I_N^{\,\text{HM}}) - \text{Var}(\hat I_N)\) as a single integral over \([a, b]\), for a general \(f\) with \(0 \le f \le c\). Can you tell its sign? For which functions \(f\) is it zero?
  3. Take \(f(x) = 4\sqrt{1 - x^2}\) on \([0, 1]\) with \(c = 4\), so that \(I = \pi\) and hit-or-miss is the circle estimator. Compute both variances. How many times more points does hit-or-miss need for the same standard error?

You should find that \(\text{Var}(\hat I_N)\) is never larger than \(\text{Var}(\hat I_N^{\,\text{HM}})\). The algebra in (b) proves this, but it does not really explain it. The explanation is in how hit-or-miss uses a point \((X, Y)\): all it asks is whether \(Y \le f(X)\). Given \(X\), the hit \(H\) is a coin flip with success probability \(f(X)/c\). So \(c\,H\) is a noisy stand-in for the height \(f(X)\): it is right on average, but it is only ever \(0\) or \(c\). Hit-or-miss therefore has two sources of randomness, the choice of \(X\) and the coin flip, whereas the sample mean has only the first, because it uses \(f(X)\) itself. The extra variance of hit-or-miss is the variance of the coin flip! The next exercise turns this observation into a proof.

Exercise 11 (Why the sample mean wins) Let \((X, Y)\) be a single uniform point in the box \([a, b] \times [0, c]\), and let \(H\) be its hit: \(H = 1\) if \(Y \le f(X)\) and \(H = 0\) otherwise. Write \[ Z^{\text{HM}} = c\,(b-a)\,H \qquad\text{and}\qquad Z = (b-a)\,f(X) \] for what this one point contributes to each estimator, so that \(\hat I_N^{\,\text{HM}}\) is the average of \(N\) independent copies of \(Z^{\text{HM}}\), and \(\hat I_N\) is the average of \(N\) independent copies of \(Z\).

  1. Show that \(\mathbb{E}[Z^{\text{HM}} \mid X] = Z\).
  2. Use the law of total variance, conditioning on \(X\), to show \(\text{Var}(Z^{\text{HM}}) \ge \text{Var}(Z)\), without computing an integral.
  3. Compute \(\mathbb{E}[\text{Var}(Z^{\text{HM}} \mid X)]\) and check that it is \(N\) times the difference you found in Exercise 10 (b).
  4. In (b) you never used the form of \(H\). State the general fact you proved.
ImportantConditioning reduces variance

If we replace a random variable by its conditional expectation, the mean stays the same and the variance goes down.

In statistics this trick is known as Rao-Blackwellization. In our case we conditioned on \(X\): the sample mean is just hit-or-miss with each hit \(H_i\) replaced by its conditional expectation \(\mathbb{E}[H_i \mid X_i] = f(X_i)/c\). Figure 5 runs both estimators on the quarter circle. Both errors fall along lines of slope \(-1/2\), and the two lines stay a fixed distance apart on the log scale. That distance is the ratio of standard errors you found in Exercise 10 (c).

What makes an integrand hard?

The standard error \((b-a)\,\sigma_f/\sqrt N\) tells us that Monte Carlo sees the integrand only through its variance. What kind of functions have a large variance? In numerical analysis we learn that smooth functions are easy to integrate and rapidly oscillating ones are hard. Is the same true here?

Exercise 12 (Monte Carlo is blind to smoothness) Let \(k\) be a positive integer and \(f(x) = \sin^2(kx)\) on \([0, \pi]\). Compute the standard error of the sample-mean estimator \(\hat I_N\) of \(\int_0^\pi f(x)\,dx\) algebraically, by evaluating the integrals for \(\sigma_f^2 = \text{Var}(f(X))\), where \(X\) is uniform on \([0, \pi]\).

You should find that the standard error is independent of the frequency \(k\). This may come as a surprise, but it makes sense once we notice that the variance \(\sigma_f^2\) depends only on the distribution of the values of \(f\), and not on the order in which these values occur along the axis. We could cut the graph into pieces and shuffle them around, and Monte Carlo would not notice.

Exercise 13 (Same values, different order) Let \(X\) be uniform on \([0, \pi]\). Explain why \(\sin^2(kX)\) has the same distribution for every nonzero integer \(k\).

Oscillation can still hurt Monte Carlo, but for a different reason.

Exercise 14 (Cancellation) Let \(f_k(x) = k \sin(kx)\) on \([0, \pi]\), with \(k\) an odd positive integer. Let \(\hat I_N\) be the sample-mean estimator of its integral with \(N\) points, and \(\sigma_{f_k}^2 = \text{Var}(f_k(X))\) for \(X\) uniform on \([0, \pi]\).

  1. Compute \(I = \int_0^\pi f_k(x)\,dx\).
  2. Compute \(\sigma_{f_k}^2\) and \(\text{SE}(\hat I_N)\). How does the standard error grow with \(k\)?
  3. By what factor is the standard error at \(k = 25\) larger than at \(k = 1\)?
  4. How many points does \(k = 25\) need for \(\text{SE}(\hat I_N) \le 0.01\)?
  5. The positive and negative lobes of \(f_k\) nearly cancel. Explain why cancellation is expensive for an average of random samples.

You should find that the standard error grows like \(k\). The integral stays at 2 for every \(k\), but the values we are averaging swing between \(-k\) and \(k\), and we are relying on them to cancel. Figure 6 shows the two cases \(k = 1\) and \(k = 25\). The slopes are the same, as they must be, and the constants differ by the factor of about 57 from part (c).

ImportantThe variance decides

For Monte Carlo, an integrand is hard when its values are spread far from their mean, compared with the size of the integral. Whether it is smooth or not plays no role.

Exercise 15 (A bound on the variance) Suppose \(m \le f(x) \le M\) on \([a, b]\). Let \(X\) be uniform on \([a, b]\) and write \(Z = f(X)\), so that \(\sigma_f^2 = \text{Var}(Z)\).

This is the exact opposite of what we saw in Exercise 12: a grid exploits smoothness, while random points ignore it.

Exercise 16 (Random against evenly spaced points) Take \(\sin x\) on \([0, \pi]\). How many random points does the sample-mean estimator need for its standard error (Exercise 8) to match the error of the midpoint rule at \(N = 100\) (Exercise 3 (a))?

You should find that 100 evenly spaced points give the same accuracy as about \(10^8\) random ones. The gap widens as \(N\) grows, because \(N^{-2}\) shrinks much faster than \(N^{-1/2}\). Many dimensions

Just like hit-or-miss, the sample mean works in any dimension. Let \(f\) again be a function on the unit cube \([0, 1]^d\), with \(I = \int_{[0,1]^d} f(x)\,dx\). Since the cube has volume 1, we simply have \(I = \mathbb{E}[f(U)]\) for \(U\) uniform on the cube.

Definition 3 (The sample-mean estimator on the cube) Given \(N\) independent uniform points \(U_1, \ldots, U_N\) in \([0, 1]^d\), estimate \(I\) by \[\hat I_N = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} f(U_i).\]

Notice that the argument of Exercise 8 never used the dimension either. So \(\hat I_N\) is unbiased with \(\text{SE}(\hat I_N) = \sigma_f/\sqrt N\), where \(\sigma_f^2 = \text{Var}(f(U))\), and the rate is \(N^{-1/2}\) in every dimension. Whatever effect the dimension has must therefore be hiding in the constant \(\sigma_f\). For the ball we saw this constant explode. Fortunately, for smooth integrands it grows far more slowly.

The constant depends on the dimension

The integral we started the chapter with is a good example, because for it we can compute exactly how the constant grows. The integrand is a product, \[ f(x) = e^{-\|x\|^2} = \prod_{k=1}^d g(x_k), \qquad g(t) = e^{-t^2}. \] Let us first see what the sample mean does with any integrand of this form, and then return to this one.

Exercise 17 (A product integrand) Let \(g\) be a function on \([0, 1]\), and write \[ \alpha = \int_0^1 g(t)\,dt, \qquad \beta = \int_0^1 g(t)^2\,dt. \] Suppose \(\alpha \ne 0\). Let \(f(x) = \prod_{k=1}^d g(x_k)\) on the cube \([0, 1]^d\). Let \(U\) be uniform on the cube, \(I = \int_{[0,1]^d} f(x)\,dx\), and \(\hat I_N\) the sample-mean estimator of \(I\) with \(N\) points (Definition 3).

  1. Show that \(I = \alpha^d\) and \(\mathbb{E}[f(U)^2] = \beta^d\). Where do you use independence?
  2. The relative standard error is \(\text{SE}(\hat I_N)/|I|\). Compute it in terms of \(d\), \(N\) and \(\rho = \beta/\alpha^2\).
  3. Show that \(\rho \ge 1\). When is \(\rho = 1\)?

Exercise 18 (The integral we started with) Take \(g(t) = e^{-t^2}\) in Exercise 17, so that \(f(x) = e^{-\|x\|^2}\). For this \(g\), \[ \alpha \approx 0.7468, \qquad \beta \approx 0.5981. \]

  1. Compute \(\rho\).
  2. How many points give a relative standard error of 1% for \(d = 1\), \(d = 10\), \(d = 20\) and \(d = 100\)?
  3. The product midpoint rule reaches 1% relative error at \(d = 10\) with \(m = 7\) points per axis. How many points is that?

You should find that the number of points needed for a fixed relative accuracy grows like \(\rho^d\). This is exponential in the dimension, just like the grid’s \(m^d\), but with a base that is barely above 1. At \(d = 10\), Monte Carlo needs about \(10^4\) points where the grid needs \(7^{10} \approx 2.8 \times 10^8\). You will often hear that Monte Carlo is immune to the curse of dimensionality. This is true of the rate but not of the constant, and how fast the constant grows depends on the integrand: like \(\rho^d\) here, and like \(1/p_d\) for the ball (Exercise 7).

Further reading: quasi-Monte Carlo

We now have two kinds of points, each with its own strength. Grid points are spread evenly, and this evenness is what gives the midpoint rule its fast rate. But a grid has to be built all at once, with \(m^d\) points, and its rate gets worse as \(d\) grows. Random points can be added one at a time in any dimension, but they clump together and leave gaps, and we pay for this with the slow rate \(N^{-1/2}\). Can we have the best of both?

In the last chapter we met a third kind of points, the Halton points, which are generated one at a time and still spread evenly. Such points are called quasi-random, and using them in place of random points in the sample-mean estimator is called quasi-Monte Carlo. For a smooth integrand, the error with \(N\) Halton points is at most a constant times \((\log N)^d/N\) (Halton 1960; Niederreiter 1992), which is almost \(N^{-1}\), compared with \(N^{-1/2}\) for random points. The app below runs all three methods on the integral of \(e^{-\|x\|^2}\) over \([0, 1]^d\) that we started the chapter with. Move the slider to change \(d\), and compare the fitted orders: the grid’s order falls like \(2/d\) and Monte Carlo’s stays at \(1/2\), whereas the Halton points keep an order near \(0.9\) all the way through ten dimensions! Notice that the quasi-Monte Carlo error is not random, so it comes with no standard error.

Summary

  • In Grids and the curse of dimensionality we derived the midpoint rule’s \(N^{-2}\) error and saw it decay to \(N^{-2/d}\) in \(d\) dimensions, where one more decimal place costs \(10^{d/2}\) times the work.
  • In Hit or miss we turned the circle estimator into an estimator of any integral. Its rate is \(N^{-1/2}\) in every dimension, but its constant explodes when the region fills a vanishing fraction of its box.
  • In The sample-mean estimator we wrote an integral as the interval’s width times an average height, found its standard error \((b-a)\,\sigma_f/\sqrt N\), and saw by conditioning that it always beats hit or miss: integrate out what you can.
  • In What makes an integrand hard? we saw that Monte Carlo sees only the variance of the values of \(f\). It is blind to smoothness and frequency, and what hurts it is cancellation. A grid uses the smoothness that random points ignore, and in one dimension it wins at every \(N\).
  • In Many dimensions we saw that the sample mean’s rate is free of the dimension but its constant is not: for a product integrand the work grows like \(\rho^d\), slowly enough that random points beat the grid within a few dimensions.

Every method in this chapter assumed a supply of uniform random numbers. The next part, Sampling, asks where they come from, and how to turn them into samples from other distributions.
